## 1. Multitask learning

Caruana (1997) introduced the idea of training a single model on several related tasks at the same time, with shared hidden layers and separate output heads for each task. In the standard setup, the lower layers of the network are shared across all tasks and the top layer branches into one head per task. The shared layers learn a representation that must be useful for every task at once. This is called hard parameter sharing.

The benefit is a form of inductive transfer. What the network learns for one task carries into the other tasks through the shared weights. Each task also acts as a regulariser for the others: the shared trunk cannot overfit to any single task's noise, because it must keep all tasks happy at the same time. Caruana showed that this consistently reduces generalisation error compared with training the same tasks separately, and that gains are largest when the tasks are related and each one has limited training data.

Our indoor positioning problem maps directly onto this idea. Building and floor classification depend on which access points are heard and how strongly, and so does position regression. In Phase 1, we trained three separate models. A shared trunk can learn from all three signals at once. The three heads are: building (3 outputs, softmax), floor (5 outputs, softmax) and coordinates (2 outputs, linear). The ablation in `docs/dl_ablations.md` partly supports this: the multitask network beats three single-task networks on position (11.08 m vs 11.50 m) and matches them on floor accuracy (82.9% vs 83.2%), but it loses 1.8 points on building accuracy (96.5% vs 98.3%).

## 2. Neural networks on UJIIndoorLoc

All three papers below use the UJIIndoorLoc data of Torres-Sospedra et al. (2014), the same training and validation files we use, so their numbers are comparable with ours.

Nowicki and Wietrzykowski (2017) used a stacked autoencoder followed by a feed-forward classifier on UJIIndoorLoc. The autoencoder compressed the 520-dimensional RSSI vector into a lower-dimensional representation in an unsupervised step, then a small classifier predicted building and floor. They tried several ways of handling the not-detected value and got their best result by replacing it with -110 dBm and scaling all inputs together, which is close to what we do (-105, then min-max). Their best configuration reached about 92% combined building-and-floor accuracy. We take from this paper the confirmation that deep features from raw RSSI can outperform k-NN on building and floor, but note they did not predict coordinates and trained two networks in sequence rather than one joint network.

Kim, Lee and Huang (2018) proposed a single scalable deep network with an autoencoder front end and a multi-label classifier for building, floor and position on UJIIndoorLoc. On UJIIndoorLoc they reported 99.82% building accuracy, 91.27% floor accuracy and a mean position error of 9.29 m, with a network small enough to run on a phone. What they showed us is that combining all three targets in one network is feasible and practical; our simpler MLP trunk replaces their autoencoder pre-training stage and is trained end-to-end with a combined loss.

Song et al. (2019), CNNLoc, combined a stacked autoencoder with a one-dimensional convolutional neural network. After scaling they set the not-detected value to 0, as we do. On the official validation file they reported 100% building accuracy and up to 96.03% floor accuracy; the setting with a mean position error of 11.78 m had 94.68% floor accuracy. We take from CNNLoc the floor accuracy level to aim for and the observation that an autoencoder pre-training step helps when inputs are sparse. Our plain MLP trunk is simpler and trained end-to-end; on the same test file we reach 92.3% floor accuracy, below CNNLoc's floor result and one point above our Phase 1 baseline (91.3%), with a lower position error (8.66 m).

## 3. Regularisation and training

Dropout (Srivastava et al., 2014) is a regularisation method that randomly sets a fraction of hidden units to zero on each forward pass during training. Because units cannot rely on specific neighbours to correct their errors, each unit must learn a useful feature on its own, which forces the network to learn redundant and independent representations. Srivastava et al. showed that a dropout rate of 0.5 was effective on many benchmark tasks. With 465 sparse inputs and about 19,000 training scans our network is at risk of memorising the training data, so we use dropout (rate 0.3) after every trunk layer. The regularisation sweep in `docs/dl_regularisation.md` confirms this: without dropout the mean position error is 12.40 m; with dropout 0.3 and weight decay it falls to 11.17 m.

Kingma and Ba (2015), Adam, is an adaptive gradient descent optimiser. Plain gradient descent uses the same learning rate for every parameter. Adam tracks a running mean and a running variance of the gradient for each parameter and divides the update by the square root of the variance. Parameters that receive large or erratic gradients get smaller steps; parameters with small or consistent gradients get relatively larger steps. This makes training more stable and faster to converge than plain SGD, particularly on sparse and high-dimensional inputs like our RSSI vectors. We use Adam with learning rate 0.001, the default suggested by Kingma and Ba.

Weight decay (Krogh and Hertz, 1992) adds a penalty on the squared size of the weights to the loss, so that every update also shrinks the weights a little. Krogh and Hertz showed that this simple penalty improves generalisation by keeping the network from relying on a few very large weights. We use weight decay 1e-4 through the Adam optimiser. In our sweep it helps most together with dropout (12.18 m with weight decay alone, 11.17 m with both).

Prechelt (1998), early stopping, monitors a validation loss during training and stops when the loss stops improving. Without early stopping a network can keep fitting the training data after the point of best generalisation: the training error keeps falling while the test error starts to rise. Prechelt compared several stopping rules and showed the trade-off between stopping early (less training time, sometimes slightly worse error) and waiting longer. We use a patience rule: a random 10% of the training scans is the validation set, and training stops when the validation loss has not improved by at least 1e-4 for 30 epochs in a row; the weights from the best epoch are kept. One lesson is our own, from `docs/dl_architecture.md` (section 2): the validation set has to be large and mixed enough to give a usable signal. Holding out whole users instead, to match our outer cross-validation, gave a validation loss so noisy that its minimum was at epoch 1, so the restored weights were always barely trained. The random split fixed this.

## 4. Hybrid ML + DL

We did not find a paper on UJIIndoorLoc that trains a Random Forest on features learned by a neural network, so our hybrid is our own construction rather than a replication. The closest published idea is HybLoc (Akram et al., 2018), already reviewed in `docs/literature_review.md`, which shows that splitting the problem (cluster the radio map, then train a forest per cluster) can beat a single global forest. Our Hybrid A (`scripts/run_hybrid.py`) splits the work between model types instead: the network trunk acts as a feature extractor and a per-building forest is trained on its 256 outputs instead of the 465 signal values. The network compresses noisy, sparse signals into a denser representation; the forest then makes axis-aligned splits in that space, on stable combinations of access points rather than on single noisy ones.

## 5. What this review tells us about our design

- Why a shared trunk: The Phase 1 baselines treated building, floor and coordinates as three independent problems. Caruana (1997) and the results in Kim et al. (2018) show that a single shared network can learn representations that serve all three targets at the same time. Our ablation partly supports this (11.08 m vs 11.50 m on position, a tie on floor, 1.8 points worse on building).
- Why dropout, weight decay and early stopping: With roughly 19,000 training scans and about 1.1 million network parameters, the network can memorise the training set. Srivastava et al. (2014), Krogh and Hertz (1992) and Prechelt (1998) are the sources for the three regularisers we use. Our sweep shows dropout and weight decay work best together.
- Why a hybrid is worth testing: The network learns a compact shared representation; a forest can exploit that representation differently from the network's own linear heads. The HybLoc idea (Akram et al., 2018) shows that splitting the problem can help a forest; we test whether splitting it between a network and a forest helps too. Our Hybrid A result (7.75 m on test) is better than either model alone on the test file, but it does not clearly beat the network in cross-validation under the project tie-break rule, and cross-validation is what we choose by.
- Floor accuracy gap: CNNLoc (Song et al., 2019) reaches up to 96.03% floor accuracy on UJIIndoorLoc. Phase 1 reached 91.3%. Our network reaches 92.3%. The gap to CNNLoc likely reflects the autoencoder pre-training and the convolutional layers they use; end-to-end training of a plain MLP trunk on a sparse input is simpler but learns less. This is a direction for future work.
